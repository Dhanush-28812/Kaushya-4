Follow one cocoon.

A silkworm spins one unbroken thread. Before it reaches the loom, its cocoon is mounted, harvested and sorted, mostly by hand. Scroll down the cocoon's journey and run the three machines we are building for each step.

The simulations are simplified models of our designs. The machines are still prototypes.

Aakar

Stage 1: mounting

Hinged hexagonal-cell panels of plastic. Set on a frame with triangular ridges they zigzag into shape and give every worm an equal cell, so cocoons come out uniform. Lifted off, they fold flat for storage.

Nivara

Stage 2: harvesting

The plastic chandrika sits on triangular ridges so it keeps equal folds. A scraper with matching triangles is pushed across by hand and the cocoons fall into the tray. Hired harvesting labour costs farmers about ₹30,000 a year, they told us.

Vidura

Stage 3: sorting

An air-lock gate releases one cocoon at a time. Three TCS34725 colour sensors and a load cell read it, an ESP32 decides, and the cocoon goes to the good or bad bin. In this model a stained shell, or one under 1.4 g, is bad.

Sericulture in five steps

  1. Egg. Incubated about 10 days until they hatch.
  2. Larva. Eats mulberry leaves for about four weeks and moults four times.
  3. Spinning. Moved to a chandrika, it spins for two to three days.
  4. Cocoon. Harvested about five to seven days after mounting, before the moth emerges.
  5. Reeling. Good cocoons are sorted and sent to be reeled into raw silk.

India is the only country producing all four commercial silks: mulberry, tasar, eri and muga. Cocoons get rejected for being double, stained, pierced, thin or misshapen.

Where our designs come from
